Analyzing Communication

Rhetorical Analysis

Every interaction within society is an open line of communication. Writers and speakers communicate within a specific context, for a specific purpose, and to a specific audience. Articles, books, movies, news reports, speeches, advertisements, and other texts all participate in these conversations.

A rhetorical analysis examines how a text communicates. The goal is not to decide whether you agree with the author. Instead, you analyze how the author attempts to achieve a purpose for an intended audience.

Why This Matters

Learning to recognize and analyze communication strategies can help you grow as a writer, reader, and critical thinker. It can also help you communicate more effectively across social, economic, and cultural boundaries.

The Rhetorical Triangle

A text's purpose is shaped by the relationship among the author, audience, and context or content. These elements should not be treated as isolated pieces. They work together to form the complete rhetorical situation.

Author

Who created the text? Consider the author's experience, perspective, position, reputation, and relationship to the topic.

Example: Martin Luther King Jr. was a Baptist minister, civil rights leader, and advocate for racial justice.

Audience

Who is the communication meant to reach? Consider what the audience already knows, believes, values, fears, or expects.

Example: The immediate audience for “I Have a Dream” included those attending the March on Washington. The broader audience included the American public, especially white Americans.

Context and Content

What is happening around the text, and what ideas does the text address? Historical, cultural, social, and political circumstances can shape how a message is created and received.

Example: King delivered the speech on August 28, 1963, during the March on Washington. The speech references the Constitution and the Emancipation Proclamation.

Purpose

What does the author want the audience to understand, believe, feel, or do after encountering the text?

Example: King sought to convince the white community and broader American public to join the Civil Rights Movement by exposing injustice and inspiring hope.

Ethos, Logos, and Pathos

Writers and speakers may use multiple rhetorical appeals at the same time. A rhetorical analysis should explain not only where an appeal appears, but also how it supports the author's purpose and affects the intended audience.

Ethos

Ethos concerns credibility and trust. Consider how the author establishes authority, knowledge, fairness, experience, or good character.

Logos

Logos concerns logic and reason. Consider how the author uses facts, examples, evidence, explanations, comparisons, or chains of reasoning.

Pathos

Pathos concerns emotion. Consider what the audience may feel and how that emotional response helps the author move toward the text's purpose.

Quick Tip

Identifying an appeal is only the beginning. Explain how the appeal would influence this particular audience and help the author achieve this particular purpose.

Questions for Analyzing a Multimodal Text

These questions may be used when analyzing a commercial, meme, short video, or another text that combines modes such as words, images, movement, and sound.

  1. Summary: What is happening in the text? What do you see, hear, or read?
  2. Audience: Who is this text aimed at? How do you know?
  3. Ethos: How does the text establish credibility or trust?
  4. Logos: How does the text use logic or reason?
  5. Pathos: How does the text appeal to emotion?
  6. Evaluation: Is the text persuasive? Why or why not? How might the appeals convince the intended audience to believe, buy, or invest in the idea or product?
  7. Source Information: Where did you find the commercial, meme, or video?

Sample Prompt from Mrs. Seay's Course

The following requirements represent one sample Rhetorical Analysis assignment. Always follow the instructions for your current course and assignment.

Length

At least five well-developed paragraphs of approximately 8–10 sentences each.

Format

MLA style, double-spaced, 12-point Times New Roman font, and one-inch margins.

Evidence

At least six direct quotations: two supporting the analysis of ethos, two for logos, and two for pathos.

Citations

Standard MLA in-text citations and a Works Cited page, even when the essay analyzes only one source.

What You Would Do

  1. Choose an assigned speech or article.
  2. Determine the author's purpose by reading and analyzing the text.
  3. Analyze how the author uses ethos, logos, and pathos to support that purpose for the intended audience.
  4. Use direct quotations and MLA citations to support the analysis.

Important Notes

  • Do not use first person or second person.
  • Do not shift into arguing whether you agree with the author's position.
  • Focus on how the speech or article attempts to achieve its purpose through rhetorical strategies.

Understanding the Conversation

Annotated Bibliography

An annotated bibliography helps you investigate a research topic before forming your argument. You research what scholars are already saying, summarize their ideas fairly, evaluate their credibility, and determine how each source contributes to your understanding of the larger conversation.

Why This Matters

A strong argument begins with understanding the existing conversation. The annotated bibliography gives you time to read, compare, summarize, and evaluate your sources before deciding exactly how your own argument will enter that conversation.

Entry Length

Each entry should contain one well-developed paragraph of approximately 6–10 sentences.

Format

MLA style, double-spaced, 12-point Times New Roman font, and one-inch margins.

Sources

One sample course assignment requires at least five peer-reviewed journal articles, including one source that presents an opposing viewpoint.

What an Annotated Bibliography Helps You Do

  • Investigate your idea: Read about the topic and discover what scholars are already saying.
  • Evaluate the evidence: Pay attention to purpose, audience, context, and credibility.
  • Understand the conversation: Summarize what has already been said instead of immediately giving your personal opinion.
  • Stay neutral: Present the source's information clearly, accurately, and logically.
  • Choose a workable topic: Select an idea with enough scholarly research and room for a reasonable argument.
  • Practice academic reading and writing: Examine how information is presented, how arguments are built, and whether a source is trustworthy.

Structure of One Entry

  1. MLA citation: Begin with the complete MLA Works Cited entry for the source.
  2. Summary: Use approximately 5–8 sentences to identify the source's thesis, main points, organization, and purpose.
  3. Evaluation: Use approximately 1–2 sentences to explain why the source is credible and how it helps you understand or research the topic.

Quick Tip

A complete annotated bibliography repeats this structure for every required source. Each source receives its own citation, summary, and evaluation.

Summary Guidance

A pure summary is a restatement of the author's ideas. It should represent the source accurately, fairly, and without adding your personal opinion.

  1. Present the source's main and supporting ideas. Leave out minor evidence, anecdotes, and counterarguments unless they are essential to understanding the text.
  2. Use clear, neutral language.
  3. Use a variety of signal verbs rather than repeatedly writing “the author states” or “the author discusses.”
  4. When discussing written texts, avoid verbs that refer primarily to audible speech, such as say, speak, and talk.
  5. Show how the source develops by using phrases such as: “She begins her article by claiming,” “She goes on to argue,” “Immediately following this claim, she demonstrates,” or “Before concluding, she indicates.”
  6. Clearly connect each source-based idea to the author by using the author's name, a pronoun, “the author,” or “the authors.”
  7. Include all important points without accidentally emphasizing only the parts you personally understand or find interesting.
  8. Do not include direct quotations in a summary.
  9. Proofread for grammar, clarity, and sentence structure.
Evaluation Guidance
  1. Explain how the source is valid for your research.
  2. Include a brief rhetorical analysis of how the author uses ethos, logos, and/or pathos to convey a purpose to an audience.
  3. Explain specifically how the source will be useful for the upcoming research project. Go beyond statements such as “I liked the source” or “It was interesting.”
  4. Do not include direct quotations in an evaluation.
  5. Proofread for grammar, clarity, and sentence structure.
Student Example: Mental Health Stigma

This sample shows one citation followed by a combined summary and evaluation paragraph. The student's wording has been preserved as an example of student work.

Bradbury, Andrew. “Mental Health Stigma: The Impact of Age and Gender on Attitudes.” Community Mental Health Journal, vol. 56, no. 5, 2020, pp. 933–938, https://doi.org/10.1007/s10597-020-00559-x.

Bradbury's purpose in this article was to discuss and evaluate the difference in the stigma around different types of mental disorders and how age groups view stigma differently. For this specific article, he chose to compare the stigma surrounding generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) and schizophrenia: those two disorders are different in perception. The author hypothesized that the younger audience was less likely to have any negative stigma towards persons with mental illness. He also stated that those of the female gender are more sympathetic towards persons with mental illness, whether dangerous or not. Slightly, the author's discussion is focused on the campaigns that are in place in the UK to help educated people about stigma and the effects that it has on someone's self-stigma and how they see their own mental illness. This study specifically covered the factors that age and gender play in how people see stigma or stigmatize different mental illnesses. While he did note that age does not affect the way that people contribute to stigma, he also found that the female gender is more sympathetic toward persons with mental illnesses. The author is well established in this field of research and provides a large amount of data to support his discussion and findings. I will use this in my paper to show that age does not make a difference when someone is experiencing or contributing to the negative stigma surrounding mental health disorders.

Entering the Conversation

Argumentative Essay

In an annotated bibliography, you explore a topic by researching what scholars are already saying. In an argumentative essay, you build on that research by taking a position and supporting it with logical reasoning and credible evidence.

College-level argumentation may require writers to address complex or controversial issues. An effective argument depends on understanding multiple viewpoints and representing them fairly, even when the writer ultimately disagrees with them.

Why This Matters

An argumentative essay is not simply about winning or attacking the other side. It is an opportunity to enter an academic conversation with evidence, reasoning, ethical source use, and respect for the audience.

Length

One sample Composition I assignment requires 6–8 well-developed paragraphs, with approximately 8–10 sentences per paragraph not including quoted evidence.

Format

MLA style, double-spaced, 12-point Times New Roman font, one-inch margins, MLA header, title, and page numbers.

Sources

One sample course assignment requires 5–7 peer-reviewed journal articles. At least one should complicate, challenge, or oppose the writer's position.

Documentation

Each source used in the essay must appear in an MLA in-text citation and on the Works Cited page.

Building the Argument

  1. Choose your claim. Decide where you stand after researching the topic. State that position clearly in the thesis and preview the main reasons supporting it.
  2. Develop your support. Use peer-reviewed sources to support the stance. Sources from the annotated bibliography may be reused.
  3. Plan for the audience. Write with readers who may disagree with you in mind. Use ethos, logos, and pathos strategically and remain professional.
  4. Address another viewpoint. Include at least one opposing or complicating source and respond through a rebuttal or acknowledgment paragraph.
  5. Integrate and cite sources. Represent every source fairly and accurately. Connect evidence directly to your own claims and reasoning.
  6. Compose and revise. Revise for focus, organization, clarity, development, tone, grammar, and MLA accuracy.

Important Notes

  • Use the same approved topic researched for the annotated bibliography when the assignments are connected.
  • Write to persuade rather than attack. Avoid insults, exaggerations, and emotional attacks.
  • Represent sources fairly and accurately.
  • Always follow the requirements and AI-use policy given for your current course and assignment.
Student Body Paragraph Example and Outline

This student example demonstrates a repeated paragraph pattern: claim, explanation, evidence, discussion, additional evidence, further discussion, and a concluding transition.

Discussion forums can be used to better help students learn collaboratively online than other instant messaging apps by encouraging students to engage and interact with certain topics in their small groups and by its structure of being knowledge constructed. Discussion forums function by letting students begin a discussion which they are encouraged to further elaborate in their small groups. According to Sun et al., “discussion forum[s] resulted in more communication aimed at knowledge construction” while “mobile instant-messaging app[s] resulted in more social interactions” (248). Sun et al. supported this claim by performing three studies which resulted with discussion forums having “2.8, 5.3 and 1.8%” socialization while instant messaging apps resulted with “42.5, 26 and 85.1%” socialization (255). Sun et al. clarify that socializing is good for students when beginning a course to help “facilitate team building” (258). That is why they recommend that once students “are familiar with each other, use discussion forums for knowledge construction” (Sun et al. 258). Discussion forums can be used to better help students learn collaboratively online because they provide less opportunities for students to veer off topic and socialize. With less opportunities for socializing, discussion forums can help students stay focused on the academic discussion presented within their small groups.

Paragraph Rhythm

Claim → Explain → Prove → Analyze → Conclude

This rhythm helps balance the writer's argument with source evidence rather than stacking quotations together.

Student Rebuttal Paragraph Example and Outline

This example acknowledges an alternative viewpoint, presents evidence supporting it, and then responds with a counterpoint and additional evidence.

Some counter this stance with their own argument that animals in all forms of captivity are treated better than wild animals. The UK's Farm Animal Council believes that animals in captivity receive better treatment than animals in the wild therefore, animals in captivity are better off. Browning and Veit mention that “there are a number of ways in which captive animals can be free and in which wild animals can be constrained” (4). They go on to list the many experiences of “freedom that animals in the wild do not have such as freedom from hunger, thirst, pain, injury, disease, discomfort, fear, distress, and freedom to perform natural behaviors” (Browing and Veit 4). Although, an agreement can be made that farm animals are treated better in captivity than in the wild, zoo and aquatic animals are not pampered like farm animals are. Farm animals are considered a food source that must get fed well, watered often, and medically supported because they will eventually become a meal on a plate. Meanwhile, zoo and aquarium animals are used for entertainment and to promote conservation. They get fed a normal diet, get watered like they would “normally” receive in the wild, and only receive medical attention in case of an emergency. This treatment is highlighted in the documentary Blackfish. Boissat et al. who are established biologists states that “Blackfish highlights the negative impacts of captivity on orcas, including a reduction in life spans, the collapse of male orcas' dorsal fins, aggression, toothaches and the separation of calves from their mothers” (1). It is true that a knowledgeable, experienced farmer can provide domesticated farm animals with the proper care they need to thrive without ever suffering. But at this time, there is no amount of training and knowledge that is going to allow an aquarium to provide for an orca the way the wild, because neglect and abuse are going to occur for no other reason than they are too large and too intelligent to be placed in a glass bowl to promote conservation. Conservation efforts cannot be achieved if the animals are suffering.

Evaluating Reasoning

Rhetorical and Logical Fallacies

A rhetorical fallacy is a flaw in reasoning. Rhetorical fallacies are tricks or illusions of thought, and writers, speakers, and media may use them to fool or manipulate an audience. They are not always easy to spot, and people may also commit them accidentally.

Rhetorical fallacies do not allow for the open, two-way exchange of ideas upon which meaningful conversations depend. Instead of relying on sound reasoning, they distract the audience through various techniques.

Emotional Fallacies

Unfairly appeal to the audience's emotions.

Ethical Fallacies

Unreasonably advance the writer's own authority or character.

Logical Fallacies

Depend on faulty logic to support a claim.

These categories can overlap. Recognizing fallacies in your own work and in the arguments of others can strengthen your analytical abilities and help you build clearer, fairer, and better-supported arguments.

Common Logical Fallacies

Ad Hominem

This Latin phrase means “against the person.” The writer attacks a person's character rather than addressing the person's argument.

Example: During the debate, the candidate attacks the opponent's personal traits rather than discussing the topic.

Ad Populum / Bandwagon

This fallacy encourages the audience to accept a claim because it is popular or supported by the majority.

Example: Facebook is used by millions of people to stay connected worldwide. Therefore, everyone should use Facebook.

Appeal to Authority

The writer relies on an authority who is not credible or qualified for the situation being discussed.

Example: Citing a children's book author as an expert on raising children when the author has never raised children.

Appeal to Ignorance

The audience is encouraged to believe a claim that lacks conclusive evidence simply because the claim has not been disproven.

Example: Treating stereotypes about Democrats or Republicans as true because no evidence has been presented to disprove a particular stereotype.

Appeal to Tradition

A claim is treated as valid simply because it follows a tradition or reflects what has been done before.

Example: Senator Bill wants to pass a new law, but it differs from what my grandfather did, so the law must be wrong.

Appeal to Pity

The writer attempts to replace relevant reasoning with an emotional appeal for sympathy.

Example: A student argues that a professor should give a higher grade because a family funeral prevented the student from completing an assignment on time.

Begging the Question

The writer expects the audience to accept the argument without providing adequate supporting evidence.

Example: People who use a Fitbit will become healthy.

Cherry Picking / Card Stacking

The writer presents only the data that supports one position while ignoring evidence that conflicts with it.

Example: Evidence A supports Person 1 and Evidence B supports Person 2. Person 1 presents only Evidence A to the audience.

Equivocation

The argument relies on a word or phrase that has multiple meanings and shifts between those meanings.

Example: Comedians may use double meanings deliberately to misdirect an audience and create humor.

False Dichotomy / Black-and-White / Either-Or

The writer presents only one or two choices as the available alternatives even though more options exist.

Example: Claiming universal healthcare is the only possible way to give everyone access to healthcare.

Guilt by Association

A person or idea is judged true or false because it is associated with another person or idea.

Example: Billy enjoys vandalizing. Suzy spends time with Billy on Fridays. Therefore, Suzy must also vandalize things.

Hasty or Sweeping Generalization

The writer reaches a broad conclusion based on an inadequate amount of evidence.

Example: Four out of five professors prefer blue pens, so professors generally prefer blue pens. The conclusion is weak because only five professors were asked.

Missing the Point

The conclusion does not logically relate to the main point of the original argument or evidence.

Example: Car thefts and student enrollment both increased during the same three months. Therefore, increased enrollment caused the car thefts.

Post Hoc / False Cause

The writer assumes that because one event happened before or near another, the first event caused the second.

Example: Walmart reduced employee wages, and unemployment later increased. Therefore, Walmart caused the increase in unemployment.

Red Herring

The writer introduces an unrelated issue to distract the audience from the main topic.

Example: A parent asks a child about declining grades, and the child quickly changes the subject.

Slippery Slope

The writer claims that one action will inevitably produce a chain of increasingly extreme consequences.

Example: Continued smoking will increase health problems, which will increase medical costs, which will then increase taxes.

Straw Man

The writer misrepresents an opponent's argument so the weakened version is easier to attack.

Example: A politician uses an exaggerated or outlandish version of an opponent's position to undermine it.

Weak or False Analogy

The argument depends on a comparison between ideas or situations that are not meaningfully alike.

Example: An apple and a tomato are both red fruits, so they must taste alike, even though one is sweet and the other is savory.

Finding a Fallacy Does Not End the Conversation

The presence of a logical fallacy does not necessarily mean that an entire argument is invalid. It means that a particular piece of reasoning is flawed or inadequately supported. Finding and correcting that flaw can make the argument stronger and easier to accept.

General Knowledge

Understanding Essay Quality

General Guidance, Not an Assignment-Specific Rubric

These descriptions provide a broad overview of characteristics that commonly distinguish different levels of academic writing. They do not replace the rubric or instructions for a particular assignment.

A-Level: Excellent Work

  • Demonstrates insightful, original, and precise argumentation.
  • Presents a clear, specific, and arguable thesis that directs the paper from beginning to end.
  • Fully supports claims with logical reasoning and relevant, well-integrated evidence.
  • Includes a thoughtful rebuttal that addresses other views respectfully and persuasively.
  • Uses strong transitions and clear paragraph structure.
  • Demonstrates strong control of formatting, tone, and grammar.
  • Leaves the reader convinced, informed, and impressed by the writing's maturity and control.

B-Level: Strong Work

  • Contains a clear, arguable thesis and solid organization.
  • Uses generally strong reasoning, although some explanations or transitions may need further development.
  • Selects and integrates evidence effectively in most places.
  • Includes an accurate rebuttal that may not fully explore every implication or counterpoint.
  • Contains only minor issues with formatting, tone, or grammar.
  • Persuades the reader and establishes credibility, although some points could be stronger.

C-Level: Average or Competent Work

  • Meets basic assignment requirements and demonstrates general understanding.
  • Includes a clear thesis that may be too broad, obvious, or underdeveloped.
  • Contains reasoning that may feel rushed or incomplete.
  • Uses evidence unevenly or connects it loosely to the writer's claims.
  • Includes a rebuttal that may be limited, unclear, or formulaic.
  • Contains noticeable errors that do not prevent comprehension.
  • Allows the reader to understand the argument but may not fully persuade the reader.

D-Level: Developing Work

  • Struggles with thesis clarity, focus, or argumentative direction.
  • Uses inconsistent or confusing organization.
  • Relies on weak, repetitive, or irrelevant evidence.
  • Includes an ineffective rebuttal or no rebuttal.
  • Contains formatting and grammar problems that distract from the meaning.
  • Leaves the reader unsure of the argument's purpose or main point.

F-Level: Unacceptable Work

  • Is missing key required components such as evidence, rebuttal, or a Works Cited page.
  • Is off-topic, plagiarized, or generated through prohibited AI use.
  • Lacks a coherent argument or understanding of the assignment.
  • Contains severe grammar, formatting, or tone problems that prevent comprehension.
  • Does not allow the reader to identify a clear claim, argument, or purpose.
