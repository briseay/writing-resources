Common Feedback I Leave

Writing Process & Mindset

  • Academic writing is a process of constant learning. The goal of feedback is to help you identify what is working, what is unclear, and what needs further development.
  • You have the foundation of the idea, but you trust too heavily that readers will be able to parse out your logic because it feels simple to you.
  • This class is about teaching you the skills of academic writing so that when you reach more complicated arguments and logic, you already know how to break down and discuss ideas because you practiced with the simpler material first.
  • The conversation here is a little informal, but the ideas are solid and there is a clear presence of critical thinking being applied.
  • Marks that do not have attached comments mean the concern has been explained elsewhere in the paper.

Coaching Direction

Encourage students to slow down, identify the exact problem, and revise the idea rather than merely editing the sentence.

Academic Tone & Conventions

  • Do not use second person in academic writing. It is informal and often accusatory in nature.
  • First person is appropriate in reflections, but avoid first-person personal and collective pronouns in other forms of academic writing.
  • Do not use first-person collective pronouns such as our, us, and we. You cannot speak for an undefined group of people.
  • Do not use contractions in academic writing.
  • We do not ask questions in academic writing because readers are unable to answer. Instead of asking a question, present the answer you want your audience to give.
  • Avoid collective or all-inclusive phrasing such as always, never, everyone, and no one. It is difficult or impossible to prove those claims within the scope of a paper.
  • Avoid weak modifiers such as very, really, a lot, and just. Most of the time, you can remove the word or replace it with a stronger, clearer choice.
  • Modifiers are often overused and add nothing of value to the sentence. Removing unnecessary modifiers can create a stronger tone and improve clarity.
  • Avoid clichés, colloquialisms, and phrasing that sounds conversational rather than academic.
  • We do not refer to authors by their first names in academic writing. Use the author's first and last name at first mention, then the last name only.
  • Avoid using verbs such as talk, say, and speak when discussing written text.
  • Authors can focus, discuss, claim, explain, and argue. Articles and sources are inanimate objects, so connect those actions to the author.
  • Avoid metadiscourse. There is no need to tell readers what you are about to do, what you are currently doing, or what you have already done in the paper.

Introductions & Conclusions

Introductions

  • Open with a discussion that is slightly broader than the thesis so you have space to develop the necessary contextual material without creating a jarring opening.
  • The introduction should move from broad to narrow, include the context readers need, and end with the thesis.
  • Too much summary in the introduction bogs down the section and takes away from the context, which should be the focus.
  • The introduction is underdeveloped. You have the broad-to-narrow concept, but the contextual discussion before the thesis needs more development.
  • The introduction includes the required components, but they are not organized in a way that creates an effective introductory conversation.
  • A complete academic paragraph is typically 8–10 sentences. A two- or three-sentence introduction is not developed enough to create an effective opening.

Conclusions

  • Begin the conclusion with a reference back to the thesis.
  • Do not merely repeat what the body paragraphs already discussed.
  • Show how the ideas or appeals came together to create the paper's larger point.
  • End by connecting the discussion to the broader academic conversation or the larger significance of the topic.
  • The conclusion is too summative and needs more development in the wrap-up and the “why this matters” side of the topic.
  • Do not introduce new evidence or direct quotations in the conclusion.
  • A two- or three-sentence conclusion is not developed enough to complete the paper effectively.

Thesis, Claims & Focus

  • The thesis should follow the structure explained in the course material and preview the main direction of the paper.
  • Each body paragraph should begin with a clear claim that identifies the focus of that section.
  • Do not begin the paragraph with someone else's idea. Begin with your claim, then use evidence to support it.
  • You have strong claims, but you double up on them in the same paragraph. Readers are not sure which claim the paragraph is going to prove.
  • You have good claims, but the paragraphs do not build into one cohesive paper. This makes the overall goal or argument difficult to follow.
  • The key sentence should identify not only the topic but the specific point the paragraph will prove.
  • To what end? What is the author or writer trying to accomplish at the heart of this discussion?

Organization & Transitions

  • Internal transition needed. These sentences are not inherently connected, which makes the points difficult to follow.
  • These ideas do not logically follow one another. There is a gap, and you need a phrase or sentence that effectively connects the thoughts.
  • Do not repeat evidence or reasoning. Each new sentence should add significance to the paragraph rather than restate what has already been said.
  • Several simple sentences are stacked on top of one another and follow the same organization. This creates a choppy rhythm.
  • Vary sentence type and organization from sentence to sentence when writing academically.
  • Streamline these thoughts. They can be combined and revised to express the same information more clearly and concisely.
  • Clunky phrasing or organization is making this idea harder to read than it needs to be.
  • Revise for concision. The same point can be made in fewer words, which will improve clarity.
  • Avoid opening every sentence with the same structure. Repeated sentence patterns make the paragraph feel rigid and choppy.

Evidence & Quote Integration

  • Every direct quotation must have a citation so readers know where the information came from.
  • This is a drop quote. Connect the quotation to your own writing by tagging it or blending it into the sentence.
  • Tagging at the end of the sentence can feel awkward and clunky. As a general rule, it is better to tag at the beginning.
  • Use a comma when the quotation flows directly after a verb of expression such as said, commented, wrote, or noted.
  • Do not use a comma when you use that to blend the quote into your own sentence.
  • When quoted material already contains quotation marks, place that material in single quotation marks inside your quotation.
  • Do not use ellipses at the beginning or end of quotations. The quotation marks already show where the direct quotation begins and ends.
  • Work with evidence in smaller chunks so readers can follow the discussion.
  • Evidence should support your ideas, not overshadow or replace them.
  • The evidence is about the topic, but it does not function as proof of the claim under discussion.
  • Evidence that merely repeats the claim does not prove the claim. It only shows that someone else is saying the same thing.
  • The evidence needs to be smoothly integrated and clearly connected to the claim it is meant to support.

The Double Down Rule

After you quote, double down. If you have one line of quoted evidence, you should generally have at least two sentences afterward in which you explain, expound upon, break down, or build on the evidence in your own words.

Reasoning & Analysis

  • Explain how. You have the foundation of the argument, but you trust too heavily that readers will automatically parse out your logic.
  • Your reasoning should explain why the claim is true or worth seriously considering.
  • The reasoning is underdeveloped. You cut the conversation short before fully breaking down why you believe what you do.
  • The reasoning should link the claim and evidence together in a way that proves the point.
  • Do not simply repeat the evidence after presenting it. Explain its significance.
  • Avoid looping phrases such as “this shows,” “this sentence gives evidence,” or “this supports the thesis.” Move directly into the reasoning instead.
  • Before moving on from this example, explain why it functions the way you claim it does and why it matters.
  • The discussion following the evidence must prove how the evidence functions in the way identified in the topic sentence.
  • The information is too vague to show readers what the source or point is actually about. Break it down specifically enough for the logic to make sense.

Rhetorical Analysis Feedback

  • Each body paragraph should identify the appeal under review, provide an example of the appeal in use, explain why the evidence functions as that appeal, and discuss how using the appeal in that way helped the author reach the audience.
  • The first sentence of each body paragraph should identify the appeal and how the author used it.
  • You discuss the appeal, but the topic sentence does not clearly establish the appeal or explain how the author used it.
  • After each piece of evidence, explain why the quotation functions as the appeal you connected it to.
  • You identified the appeal and included evidence, but the discussion does not explain how or why the author used the appeal in that way.
  • Having a specific audience identification makes the discussion about why the author used the appeal more developed.
  • Add why the appeal would have worked for the audience. Without the answer to that question, the rhetorical analysis is not fully connected to audience or purpose.
  • For pathos, identify the specific emotion the author wanted the audience to feel. Without naming the emotion, the discussion cannot fully explain how or why the appeal works.
  • You shift into discussing the topic of the article instead of analyzing how and why the author used the appeal. This creates a summative analysis rather than a rhetorical analysis.
  • The conclusion should examine how the appeals worked together to create a successful argument.

Questions to Prompt Stronger Rhetorical Analysis

  • Why does this quotation function as ethos, logos, or pathos?
  • What specific audience is the author trying to reach?
  • Why would this appeal work for that audience?
  • How does the appeal help the author achieve the purpose?
  • What specific emotion is the author trying to create?
  • How do the appeals build on one another?

Annotated Bibliography Feedback

  • Each entry should contain one paragraph that begins with 5–8 sentences of pure summary, followed by one evaluation sentence and one sentence explaining how the source will be used in the upcoming paper.
  • All summary sentences should be clearly connected to the author by name, pronoun, or phrase so readers can distinguish the author's ideas from your own evaluation.
  • The summary reads more like a description of the topic than a summary of the author's ideas.
  • A description tells readers what the article is about. A summary shows what the author argues, explains, and concludes.
  • Focus on what the author says rather than merely describing what the author did.
  • The summary is too vague to show what the author actually discussed.
  • The summary feels like a data dump. Focus on the author's overall point and how the author supports it rather than including every fact or statistic.
  • The first evaluation sentence should address the quality of the source based on the author's credentials or the significance of the findings within the larger academic conversation.
  • The final sentence should explain a specific plan for how the source will be used in the argumentative essay.
  • The evaluation is too vague. Explain specifically how the author used ethos, logos, or pathos and why that use supports the source's credibility.

Summaries and Source Wording

Summaries cannot include word-for-word pulls from the source. Changing a few words is paraphrasing, not summarizing. In a pure summary, the ideas remain the author's, but the wording, sentence structure, and phrasing should be entirely your own. Word-for-word source language functions as a direct quotation and becomes a citation problem when it is not formatted and documented as one.

Description vs. Summary

Description: “This article talks about how social media affects teenagers.”

Summary: “The author argues that social media increases anxiety among teenagers by encouraging constant comparison, drawing on survey data from over 2,000 students.”

Argumentative Essay Feedback

  • Begin each body paragraph with a clear claim that supports the thesis.
  • Reasons should explain why the claim should be seriously considered by readers.
  • Evidence should prove the claim rather than merely repeat it.
  • Use specific evidence that bolsters the argument without overshadowing it or replacing your voice.
  • Double down after every piece of evidence so the paragraph does not become dense with data.
  • The rebuttal should acknowledge the opposing idea, provide evidence supporting that view, present your response, and support your response with evidence.
  • A rebuttal is underdeveloped when it merely mentions an opposing idea without fully explaining or responding to it.
  • The response to the opposing view must address the same issue raised by the opposing source.
  • A rebuttal without evidence is unsupported and does not function academically.
  • The conclusion should refer back to the thesis, wrap up the argument without becoming repetitive, and connect the discussion to a larger academic conversation.

Questions to Prompt Stronger Argumentation

  • What exactly is the claim in this paragraph?
  • Why should a skeptical reader accept that claim?
  • How does the evidence prove the claim rather than repeat it?
  • What does the opposing view actually argue?
  • Does the response address the same issue?
  • Why does this argument matter beyond the paper?

Grammar & Sentence Clarity

  • Introductory comma here. When a sentence or second clause opens with a dependent clause, phrase, or word, set it apart from the independent clause with a comma.
  • No comma is usually needed when the dependent clause or phrase appears at the end of the sentence.
  • Use a comma before a coordinating conjunction when it joins two complete sentences.
  • Do not use a comma with the coordinating conjunction when it creates only a compound subject or compound predicate.
  • A comma splice occurs when two complete sentences are joined with only a comma. Correct it with a period, semicolon, comma plus coordinating conjunction, or by making one clause dependent.
  • A run-on occurs when two complete sentences are connected with nothing. Use a comma plus coordinating conjunction, semicolon, period, or dependent clause structure.
  • A fragment is a phrase or dependent clause attempting to stand alone as a complete sentence.
  • When joining two independent clauses with a conjunctive adverb, use a semicolon before the conjunctive adverb and a comma after it.
  • Subjects and verbs should match in number and person.
  • This pronoun has an unclear antecedent and could refer to more than one noun. Revise so readers know exactly what the pronoun refers to.
  • Maintain one verb tense as much as possible to improve readability and flow.
  • Use simple verb tenses when possible. Overcomplicated or conditional verb constructions can bog down clarity.
  • Parallel ideas should be expressed in parallel form. Similar ideas and functions should use similar grammatical structures.
  • Passive voice hides the actor and weakens clarity. Name who or what is performing the action when possible.
  • The only time a noun is possessive is when it owns a noun that follows it. Nouns do not own verbs.
  • We generally avoid ending sentences in prepositions in academic writing, though this convention is becoming less strict.
  • This preposition does not convey the correct relationship between the noun or verb and the prepositional phrase.

MLA & Source Use

  • Every in-text citation must match the first element of the Works Cited entry.
  • Every quote needs a citation unless the author's name is clearly included in a tag phrase and the citation requirements are otherwise satisfied.
  • Use the author's last name in academic writing and in parenthetical citations.
  • A Works Cited page begins on a separate page after the essay.
  • Entries should use hanging indents, be double-spaced, and not include extra spacing between entries.
  • Include a DOI for online academic sources when available. If no DOI is available, include the URL.
  • Use title case: capitalize the first word, last word, and all major words in between.
  • Short works such as articles use quotation marks. Journals, books, and other containers use italics.
  • For three or more authors, use the first author's name followed by et al. For two authors, include both authors.
  • Do not add extra spacing between paragraphs or Works Cited entries.
  • An acronym should be written out and followed by the acronym in parentheses the first time it appears. The acronym may be used by itself after that.

Questions I Use to Push the Thinking

Revision Coaching Questions

  • How so?
  • Why does this matter?
  • To what end?
  • What is the author trying to accomplish at the heart of this?
  • What specific audience is being addressed?
  • Why would this appeal work for that audience?
  • What specific emotion is the author trying to create?
  • How does this evidence prove the claim?
  • What does the quotation add that the claim does not already say?
  • What logical step are you asking the reader to make?
  • Can this be stated more directly?
  • Are these two sentences logically connected?
  • What belongs between these ideas so readers can follow the movement?
  • Is this describing the topic, or summarizing the author's ideas?
  • Are you explaining what the author said, or merely what the author did?
  • What makes this source credible beyond simply being peer reviewed?
  • How will this source specifically be used in the upcoming paper?
  • Does the rebuttal respond to the same issue raised by the opposing source?
  • How do the ideas or appeals work together?
  • How does the conclusion connect the paper to a broader academic conversation?

Positive Feedback Language

General

  • Great content here. You included all the needed components and organized them effectively.
  • Your overall tone is professional and appropriate for this type of paper.
  • No conventions were broken, and the tone was appropriate for this level of writing.
  • The ideas are solid, and there is a clear presence of critical thinking.
  • You have a solid foundation of writing skills.

Rhetorical Analysis

  • You have clear and fully developed discussions showing how the author used each appeal to connect to the audience.
  • Your discussion connecting the appeal to the success of the author's purpose was well done.
  • All key sentences are present, identify the appeal, and appear at the beginning of the paragraph.

Argumentative Writing

  • Strong, clear claims throughout support the thesis and focus each paragraph.
  • Clear reasoning is logically connected to the claims, and there are no gaps in the logic.
  • The evidence supports the claims and furthers the discussion without becoming repetitive.
  • You chose specific evidence that bolstered the argument without overshadowing it or replacing your voice.
  • Really solid rebuttal. It is balanced, complete, and includes the needed components on both sides of the conversation.
  • The conclusion includes the key components and avoids becoming entirely summative.
