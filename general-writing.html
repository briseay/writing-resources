Academic Writing Expectations

Guidelines commonly used in Composition I

These are the most frequent habits that weaken student writing. Use this list as a quick reference guide while drafting and revising your papers.

1. No First Person

Avoid I, me, my, our, we, and us. Avoid phrases such as “I think,” “I feel,” and “I believe.”

Your name is already on the paper. Your argument is your perspective.

I feel that this claim is accurate based on the evidence.
→
This claim is accurate based on the evidence.

2. No Second Person

Avoid you, your, and yours. Academic writing is not a conversation. Second person sounds informal and bossy.

3. No Contractions

Contractions are too casual. Spell words out fully.

Scholars couldn't determine if the trial was successful.
→
Scholars could not determine if the trial was successful.

4. Avoid Overusing “Like”

Overusing “like” makes writing sound conversational instead of formal.

5. Avoid Informal Speech or Clichés

Do not write how you talk to friends. Cut out phrases such as “at the end of the day” or “a matter of life and death.”

6. No Text Lingo or Shortened Words

Write out words fully: because, not bc; with, not w/.

7. Use Active Voice, Not Passive Voice

Active voice is clearer and stronger. The subject does the action.

Active: I baked the cake.

Passive: The cake was baked by me.

8. Do Not Ask Questions

Readers cannot answer. Instead of writing a question, write the answer you want them to understand.

9. Common Knowledge vs. Cited Information

Common knowledge is widely known and used without citation.

Rule of thumb: If five or more sources use the same fact with no citation, it is common knowledge.

Quick Tip

When in doubt, cite it.

10. No Quotes in Introductions or Conclusions

Start and end with your words.

  • Introduction: context and thesis
  • Conclusion: wrap-up and big picture, not new evidence or someone else's words

Quick Checklist Before You Submit

  • No first or second person
  • No contractions
  • No text lingo
  • Formal tone with no casual speech
  • Active voice
  • No unanswered questions
  • All facts cited unless they are truly common knowledge
  • Quotes only in body paragraphs

Clarity and Concision

Writing that is clear and polished makes your ideas stronger. Use this section as a quick reference while drafting and revising.

Be Direct

Avoid extra words and filler.

Due to the fact that
→
Because

Cut Redundancies

Do not say the same thing twice.

Each and every student must...
→
Each student must...

Choose Precise Words

Be specific instead of vague.

Things, stuff, a lot
→
Concepts, materials, several

Avoid Clutter

Shorter, stronger sentences are better than long, confusing ones.

Quick Tip

Read each sentence and ask whether every word is necessary. Clear writing does not need extra padding.

Mechanics Check

  • Check spelling and grammar. Use tools, but also read carefully.
  • Fix fragments. Every sentence needs a subject and verb.
  • Fix run-ons. Break apart long sentences.
  • Use commas for clarity and end sentences correctly.
  • Use apostrophes for possession, not plurals.
  • Use consistent verb tense. Past stays past; present stays present.

Fragment: Because the study was difficult.

Complete sentence: The study was difficult.

Run-on: The class was long it felt endless.

Corrected: The class was long, and it felt endless.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • My sentences are clear and concise.
  • I cut wordiness and vague language.
  • I fixed fragments and run-ons.
  • My punctuation is correct.
  • My verb tense is consistent.

Writing Introductions

An introduction should tell your reader exactly what your paper is about and how it is structured. Introductions are usually 5–10% of the length of a typical essay. Readers gain their first impressions of a paper from this section, so an effective introduction is vital.

Generally, introductions begin broad and connect the topic of the paper with some larger idea or concept.

Then, an introduction provides readers with brief background or contextual information that provides a focus for the paper.

This should be followed by a clear articulation of your controlling argument or point of view, a sentence that is sometimes called a thesis statement.

The thesis statement must directly and clearly respond to the set question or topic.

What the Introduction Should Tell Your Reader

  • How you understand the topic, including context, background, or key terms
  • What your controlling argument is
  • What issues you will cover and in which order
  • Which ideas are major or minor in longer essays
  • What conclusion you will reach

Quick Tip

Writing an essay is not like writing a mystery novel with an unfolding plot. Aim to be transparent and direct.

Your reader should not have to read several pages before finding your argument or thesis. It needs to appear in the introduction.

Do not pad your introduction with too much detail or references from other sources. Relate this section directly to the set question or topic.

Things to Avoid

The “Definition” Introduction

Some students begin papers with a sentence such as, “Webster's Dictionary defines ‘term’ as...” This technique is dry and overused.

If you must provide a definition, make sure the information is absolutely essential and directly relates to the point of the essay.

The “Restatement” Introduction

If your professor gave you an assignment prompt, you may be tempted to restate the question as your introduction opener.

Remember that your professor is already aware of the assignment. Merely restating the prompt will usually produce an ineffective introduction.

“In This Paper, I Will Be Discussing...”

Because the reader is aware that you wrote the paper, it is unnecessary to remind them.

This phrase is very common and boring. Do your best to avoid it.

Writing Summaries

  1. Pure summaries do not include your personal opinion or analysis of the passage. They are simply a restatement of the author's ideas.
  2. Present only the main and supporting ideas in the text. Leave out supporting evidence, anecdotes, and counterarguments unless they are crucial to understanding the text.
  3. Describe the text accurately and fairly by using neutral language.
  4. Use a variety of signal verbs, not just “he discusses” or “the author states” repeatedly.
  5. Avoid verbs such as say, speak, and talk, as well as other verbs that refer to audible sound, when summarizing written text rather than spoken words such as speeches.
  6. Frame your summary with phrases that indicate how the article or speech is organized and how it moves from point to point.
  7. Make sure your summary phrases include direct and clear links to the author so you do not convey that an idea is yours when it is not.
  8. Connect to the author by using the author's name, a pronoun, or the phrase “the author” or “the authors.”
  9. Make sure you include all the important points and express them clearly.
  10. Make sure you are not accidentally distorting the source by stressing what you understand rather than what the original source emphasizes.
  11. Do not include quotes in a summary.
  12. Finally, proofread your summary by checking for grammar errors and sentence structure and variety.

Helpful Organizational Phrases

  • “She begins her article by claiming...”
  • “She goes on to argue...”
  • “Before concluding, she indicates...”
  • “Immediately following this claim, she demonstrates...”

Examples of Summaries

Bad Example of a Summary

Martin Luther King Jr. gives a speech about how he wants freedom for Black people and he hopes everyone will be equal one day. He says “I have a dream” a lot and it's really inspiring. I think he makes good points about racism being bad and how people should get along.

Good Example of a Summary

In his speech “I Have a Dream,” Martin Luther King Jr. urges the American people to recognize the failures of the nation in living up to its promise of equality. He begins by referencing the Emancipation Proclamation and arguing that true freedom has not yet been realized for African Americans. King emphasizes the injustices of segregation and discrimination while insisting on the urgency of change. He goes on to appeal to hope, using the repeated phrase “I have a dream” to describe his vision of racial harmony and justice. King concludes by pointing to faith and perseverance as essential tools in achieving freedom and equality for all.

Teacher's Note: Notice how the bad example includes personal opinion (“I think...”) and vague generalizations, leaving out the speech's structure and main ideas. The good example clearly summarizes King's argument step by step, stays objective, and avoids quoting directly.

Transition Sentences

Why Do I Need to Use Transition Sentences?

  • They bring out the logical relationship between ideas, sentences, paragraphs, and sections of a paper.
  • They provide the reader with directions for how to piece together your ideas into a logically coherent argument.
  • They make your writing sound less choppy, jumpy, or abrupt and help establish flow.

What Are the Components of Good Transition Sentences?

  • They make an explicit connection between ideas, sentences, and paragraphs.
  • They use specific words instead of vague pronouns such as “this” to clarify meaning.

External Transitions Between Paragraphs

External transitions highlight the relationship between the two main ideas of each paragraph.

Good Example

Paragraph 1: High school gives students structure and support by providing daily schedules, reminders from teachers, and frequent guidance from parents. This constant oversight helps students stay on track with their assignments and responsibilities.

Paragraph 2: While high school prepares students with basic academic skills, college requires a higher level of independence and time management. In college, students must create their own schedules, balance coursework with jobs or extracurricular activities, and take initiative to seek help from professors.

Bad Example

Paragraph 1: High school gives students structure and support by providing daily schedules, reminders from teachers, and frequent guidance from parents.

Paragraph 2: College is different. Students in college must be responsible for their own time and choices.

Internal Transitions Within Paragraphs

Internal transitions are short words or phrases that help the reader anticipate what they will read next and guide them from sentence to sentence.

Good Example

For example, one major health effect of fast food is the increase in obesity rates. In addition, regular consumption of fast food has been linked to higher risks of diabetes and heart disease.

Bad Example

For example, one major health effect of fast food is the increase in obesity rates. Another thing is fast food is bad.

Proofreading for Transitions

  • Check whether the end of each paragraph connects to the first sentence of the next.
  • If there is no clear connection, clarify your logic or rearrange the paragraphs.

Writing Conclusions

A conclusion should bring together different sections of your essay.

The assertions you made in your introductory paragraph should have been fully developed and substantiated through the essay so that the conclusion can bring together all strands of the argument, refer back to the essay topic, and end on a well-reasoned, logical note.

Remember

Your conclusion should not offer any new material.

Instead, consider telling your reader:

  • What the significance of your findings or the implications of your conclusion might be
  • How your topic links to the wider context or “bigger picture” in your discipline

Do not simply repeat yourself in this section. A conclusion that merely summarizes is repetitive and reduces the impact of the paper.

Key Structural Elements of Introductions and Conclusions

The introduction moves from general information to specific information, while the conclusion works in reverse, moving from specific information to general information.

The Introduction

  1. Brief background or contextual information
  2. Focus attention on the specific theme of the writing
  3. Thesis statement outlining the main contention or argument
  4. Elaboration on organization or structure

The Conclusion

  1. Refer back to the question and thesis statement
  2. Wrap up the ideas presented in the body paragraphs
  3. Connect to the larger academic conversation
