Grammar

Grammar becomes easier when you stop treating it as a pile of unrelated rules. Sentences are built from clauses. Once you understand that structure, punctuation, sentence boundaries, consistency, and revision begin to fit together.

Sentence Structure First, Punctuation Second

Most sentence-level grammar errors are really sentence boundary problems. This guide begins with clauses, subjects, and predicates, then moves into fragments, run-ons, commas, semicolons, agreement, apostrophes, and revision choices.

You do not need to memorize everything at once. Start with the section that matches the problem you are trying to solve.

Build a Sentence

Start with the pieces every sentence depends on.

What Is a Clause?

A clause is a group of words that contains a subject and a predicate. Every complete sentence must contain at least one clause.

Phrases

A phrase is a group of related words that is missing a subject, a predicate, or both.

Phrases add description or detail, but they cannot stand alone as sentences.

Clauses

A clause contains both a subject and a predicate.

Clauses are the units sentences are built from.

Example

Sentence: When the paper was finished, the student, impressed by her determination and willpower, turned it in to Turnitin.

Phrases:

  • impressed by her determination and willpower
  • to Turnitin

Clauses:

  • When the paper was finished
  • the student turned it in
Key Takeaway Sentences are built from clauses, not just words.
Subjects and Predicates

Every clause has two essential parts: a subject and a predicate.

Subject

The subject tells who or what the clause is about.

  • Simple subject: the main word or words doing the action
  • Complete subject: the simple subject plus words that describe or modify it
Example

The bright copper coin sparkled on the sidewalk.

  • Simple subject: coin
  • Complete subject: The bright copper coin

Predicate

The predicate tells what the subject is doing or being.

  • Simple predicate: the verb or verb phrase
  • Complete predicate: the verb plus all words that modify or complete its meaning
Example

The microscopic dust mite was discovered less than three decades ago.

  • Simple predicate: was discovered
  • Complete predicate: was discovered less than three decades ago
The Tense-Shift Test Change the time of the sentence. What changes is the verb.
Key Takeaway If you can find the subject and predicate, you can find the clause.
Independent and Dependent Clauses

Independent Clauses

An independent clause has a subject and predicate, expresses a complete thought, and can stand alone.

  • The students revised their essays.
  • The experiment failed.

Dependent Clauses

A dependent clause has a subject and predicate but begins with a word that makes it depend on another clause. It does not express a complete thought.

  • When the experiment failed...
  • Because the students revised their essays...

Words That Create Dependence

  • Subordinating conjunctions: because, although, while, since, after, before, if, when, though
  • Relative pronouns: who, whom, whose, which, that
Key Takeaway A dependent clause cannot stand alone as a sentence, even though it contains a subject and a verb.

Build Better Sentences

Understand how clauses combine and how sentence boundary errors happen.

Sentence Types

Simple Sentences

A simple sentence contains one independent clause. It may still have compound subjects or predicates.

  • Sylvia swam.
  • Sylvia and Joseph swam.
  • The trailer sits on the edge of a pond and is surrounded by a wooden deck.

Compound Sentences

A compound sentence contains two or more independent clauses joined by a comma plus FANBOYS or by a semicolon.

  • Cecelia walks, and Martha bikes.
  • The cell was large; it could attach to the molecule.

Complex Sentences

A complex sentence contains one independent clause and at least one dependent clause.

  • When the experiment failed, the team regrouped.
  • The bicycle, which was in the street, is missing.

Optional: Compound-Complex Sentences

A compound-complex sentence contains two or more independent clauses and at least one dependent clause.

I complimented Joe when he finished the job, and he seemed pleased.
Key Takeaway Sentence types are not about length. They are about how clauses connect.
Fragments

A fragment is a group of words written as a sentence that does not contain a complete independent clause.

Common Causes

  • A dependent clause is written as a sentence.
  • A phrase is mistaken for a complete thought.
Fragment Examples
  • Because the experiment failed.
  • When the students revised their essays.
  • Running down the hallway.
  • To complete the assignment on time.

How to Fix Fragments

  1. Attach the fragment to a nearby independent clause.
  2. Rewrite it to include an independent clause.
Corrected
  • Because the experiment failed, the scientist tried another idea.
  • The instructor was happy when the students revised their essays.
Key Takeaway If a sentence does not contain at least one independent clause, it is a fragment.
Run-On Sentences

A run-on sentence occurs when two or more independent clauses are joined without proper punctuation or connection.

  • Run-ons are not determined by length.
  • Short sentences can be run-ons.
  • Long sentences can be correct.
Run-On The experiment failed the team revised their hypothesis.

How to Fix Run-Ons

  1. Separate the clauses into two sentences.
  2. Join them with a comma and FANBOYS.
  3. Join them with a semicolon.
  4. Use dependent clause structure.
Corrected
  • The experiment failed. The team revised their hypothesis.
  • The experiment failed, and the team revised their hypothesis.
  • The experiment failed; the team revised their hypothesis.
  • When the experiment failed, the team revised their hypothesis.
Key Takeaway Run-ons happen when independent clauses are not properly separated.
Comma Splices

A comma splice occurs when two independent clauses are joined using only a comma.

Comma Splice The experiment failed, the team revised their hypothesis.

A comma is not strong enough to join independent clauses by itself.

How to Fix a Comma Splice

  1. Add a coordinating conjunction.
  2. Replace the comma with a semicolon.
  3. Separate the clauses into two sentences.
Corrected
  • The experiment failed, and the team revised their hypothesis.
  • The experiment failed; the team revised their hypothesis.
  • The experiment failed. The team revised their hypothesis.
Key Takeaway A comma alone cannot join independent clauses.

Use Punctuation with Purpose

Commas and semicolons are structural tools, not decorative pauses.

Commas with Independent Clauses: FANBOYS

Use a comma before a coordinating conjunction when it joins two independent clauses.

FANBOYS

for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so

  • The title sounds impressive, but the job is mostly clerical.
  • The experiment failed, so the team revised their hypothesis.
Incorrect The experiment failed, the team revised their hypothesis.
How to Check
  1. Are there two independent clauses?
  2. Is a coordinating conjunction joining them?
  3. Is the comma placed before that conjunction?
Key Takeaway A comma can join independent clauses only with FANBOYS.
Commas with Introductory Elements

Commas often signal the end of an introductory phrase or clause before the main idea begins.

Introductory Dependent Clauses

  • When the experiment failed, the team regrouped.
  • Because the students revised their essays, the final drafts improved.

Introductory Phrases

  • After several revisions, the essay was ready to submit.
  • In the early morning hours, the campus was quiet.

When a Comma Is Usually Not Needed

  • The team regrouped when the experiment failed.
  • The students revised their essays because the feedback was clear.
Key Takeaway Introductory elements use commas to prevent confusion, not to create pauses.
Semicolons

A semicolon connects closely related independent clauses without using a conjunction.

  • The experiment failed; the team revised their hypothesis.
  • She loves research; writing is her strength.

Semicolon vs. Comma vs. Period

  • Comma: weak boundary that needs help
  • Semicolon: strong boundary
  • Period: complete separation
Incorrect When the experiment failed; the scientists revised their hypothesis.

Semicolons with Conjunctive Adverbs

Common conjunctive adverbs include however, therefore, meanwhile, moreover, consequently, nevertheless, and instead.

The data was incomplete; however, the trend was still visible.
Key Takeaway Semicolons join independent clauses when ideas are closely connected.

Keep Your Writing Consistent

Verb and pronoun consistency helps readers follow your meaning.

Verb Tense Consistency

Verb tense tells readers when something is happening. Unnecessary shifts can confuse readers even when individual sentences are grammatically correct.

Common Academic Patterns

  • Present tense for analysis, arguments, and general truths
  • Past tense for completed studies, experiments, or historical events
Inconsistent The author argues that social media was harmful to teenagers and explains why it affects mental health.
Revised The author argues that social media is harmful to teenagers and explains why it affects mental health.

Tense shifts may be appropriate when quoting a source, describing historical events, or moving between analysis and summary. The shift should be intentional.

Key Takeaway Consistent verb tense helps readers focus on ideas instead of sentence mechanics.
Subject-Verb Agreement

Subjects and verbs must agree in number.

  • The student writes carefully.
  • The students write carefully.

Common Challenges

  • Interrupting words: The list of required readings is long.
  • Subjects joined by and: The professor and the advisor agree.
  • Subjects joined by or/nor: The verb agrees with the closest subject.
  • Indefinite pronouns: Everyone needs to submit the assignment.
Key Takeaway Identify the true subject before choosing the verb.
Pronoun-Antecedent Clarity

An antecedent is the noun a pronoun refers to. Readers should be able to identify that noun clearly.

The student submitted the essay because she wanted feedback.
Unclear When the students met with the professor, they were frustrated.
Clear When the students met with the professor, the students were frustrated.

Agreement and Revision

Pronouns should match their antecedents in number.

  • Each student must submit his or her assignment.
  • Students must submit their assignments.

Avoid Vague Pronouns

Unclear This shows why the policy failed.
Clear This result shows why the policy failed.
Key Takeaway Pronoun clarity matters more than grammatical shortcuts.

Small Marks, Big Difference

Apostrophes have two jobs: possession and contractions.

Apostrophes for Possession

Apostrophes show that one thing belongs to another.

Singular Possession

Add 's to singular nouns.

  • the student's essay
  • the dog's leash
  • James's notebook

Plural Possession

Add an apostrophe after the s if the plural noun already ends in s.

  • the students' essays
  • the teachers' lounge

If the plural noun does not end in s, add 's.

  • children's books
  • people's opinions
How to Check Ask: Who owns what?
Key Takeaway Apostrophes show ownership, not quantity.
Apostrophes for Contractions

Apostrophes in contractions show where letters have been removed.

  • do not → don't
  • it is → it's
  • they are → they're
  • cannot → can't

Its vs. It's

  • it's = it is
  • its = possessive
  • It's important to proofread your essay.
  • The company changed its policy.
Apostrophes Do Not Make Nouns Plural
  • Incorrect: apple's
  • Correct: apples
  • Incorrect: student's need to submit essays
  • Correct: students need to submit essays
Quick Test Try expanding the word into a contraction. If it does not make sense, you probably do not need an apostrophe.

Polish Your Writing

These are revision choices rather than simple right-or-wrong grammar rules.

Active and Passive Voice

Active and passive voice both have legitimate uses, but academic writing strongly prefers active voice because it is clearer and more direct.

Active Voice

The subject performs the action.

The researcher analyzed the data.

Passive Voice

The subject receives the action.

The data was analyzed.

Passive voice may be useful when the actor is unknown or irrelevant, when the result deserves emphasis, or when disciplinary conventions allow it.

Revision Strategy
  1. Who is performing the action?
  2. Can that actor be named?
  3. Would the sentence be clearer in active voice?
Concision

Concision means expressing ideas clearly and efficiently. It removes unnecessary words that hide meaning.

Common Causes of Wordiness

  • Redundant phrases
  • Inflated expressions
  • Unnecessary qualifiers

Wordy: Due to the fact that

Concise: Because

Redundant: basic fundamentals

Redundant: past history

Revision Questions
  • Can this idea be stated more directly?
  • Are any words repeating the same meaning?
  • Would removing words change the meaning?
Key Takeaway Concision sharpens ideas. It does not weaken them.
Sentence Variety

Sentence variety uses different sentence lengths and structures to keep writing clear and engaging.

  • Too many short sentences feel choppy and flatten emphasis.
  • Too many long sentences blur ideas and overwhelm readers.

Ways to Create Variety

  • Combine related ideas.
  • Vary sentence openings.
  • Mix simple, compound, and complex sentences.
Monotonous The study was conducted last year. The results were surprising. The data was incomplete.
Revised The study was conducted last year, and although the results were surprising, the data was incomplete.
Parallelism

Parallel structure uses the same grammatical pattern to show that ideas are equal in importance.

Common Parallel Structures

  • Items in a list
  • Paired ideas
  • Comparisons
Parallel She enjoys reading, writing, and researching.
Not Parallel She enjoys reading, writing, and to research.
Revision Strategy When ideas are connected, check that they use the same grammatical form and maintain a consistent structure.

Quick Reference

Use these resources when you need clarification, not as lists to memorize.

Capitalization

Capitalize:

  • Proper nouns
  • Specific titles used with names
  • Major words in titles of works

Do not capitalize:

  • Common nouns
  • General directions
  • Seasons
  • Academic subjects, except languages

Key takeaway: Capitalize specific names, not general categories.

Italics vs. Quotation Marks

Use italics for long or standalone works:

  • Books
  • Movies
  • TV series
  • Journals and newspapers
  • Albums

Use quotation marks for shorter works:

  • Articles and essays
  • Short stories and poems
  • TV episodes
  • Songs

Commonly Confused Words

  • affect = verb; effect = noun
  • its = possessive; it's = it is
  • there / their / they're
  • your / you're
  • then / than

Quick check:

  1. Substitute the meaning.
  2. Read the sentence aloud.
  3. Look it up. There is no penalty for checking.
