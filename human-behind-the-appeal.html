The Human Behind the Appeal

Rhetorical analysis is not just about finding ethos, logos, and pathos. It is about understanding why a rhetorical choice might work on an actual human being.

Scenario 1 of 5

Before You Begin

You already know how to identify ethos, logos, and pathos. In this activity, we are going one step further.

When writers make rhetorical choices, they are making assumptions about the people they hope to persuade. Your job as a rhetorical analyst is to uncover those assumptions.

Choice → Audience → Human Response → Effect

Another way to think about it is to ask: What is the writer banking on about people?

Each scenario will begin by asking you to identify the audience. Keep that audience in mind as you work. A rhetorical choice only makes sense when we consider who is receiving it.

Scenario 1: Training Wheels

Relevant Expertise

A pediatrician is speaking to a group of parents about childhood nutrition. Early in the presentation, she explains that she has practiced pediatric medicine for 18 years.

First: Who Is the Audience?

Who is the primary audience in this scenario?

Step 1: Choice

What rhetorical choice did the speaker make?

Step 2: Audience

Why is this particular information relevant to this particular audience?

Step 3: Human Response

What is the speaker banking on about human behavior?

Step 4: Effect

How could this human response help the speaker accomplish her purpose?

You Just Moved from Identification to Analysis

It would have been easy to stop with: "The speaker mentions her medical experience, so this is ethos."

That is identification. It is not yet rhetorical analysis.

Choice → Audience → Human Response → Effect

The speaker establishes her medical experience → she is speaking to parents seeking information about their children's health → people often give greater trust to someone with relevant expertise when dealing with specialized information → the parents may therefore be more willing to trust and seriously consider her recommendations.

That is the move we are practicing.

Scenario 2: Shared Experience

Connection as Credibility

A college graduate is speaking to a group of first-generation college students about adjusting to college. Early in the talk, the speaker says, "When I started college, no one in my family had been through this before. During my first semester, I felt completely lost too."

First: Who Is the Audience?

Who is the primary audience in this scenario?

Step 1: Choice

What rhetorical choice did the speaker make?

Step 2: Audience

Why might sharing this particular experience matter to this particular audience?

Step 3: Human Response

What is the speaker banking on about human behavior?

Step 4: Effect

How could this response help the speaker accomplish the purpose of helping students adjust to college?

Ethos Does Not Require a Résumé

Choice → Audience → Human Response → Effect

The speaker shares an experience of feeling lost as a first-generation college student → the audience consists of first-generation students adjusting to college → people may be more receptive to someone they believe understands an experience they have personally faced → the students may therefore be more willing to listen to the speaker's advice.

Notice what happened to ethos here.

The speaker never gave us a job title, years of professional experience, or a formal credential. Credibility can also develop through connection. For this audience, shared experience may help establish the speaker as someone worth listening to.

Scenario 3: Reframing an Emotion

Pathos Is More Than Making Someone Sad

A college tutoring center is creating a message for students who may be hesitant to ask for academic help. The message says: "Asking for help doesn't mean you're failing. It means you care enough about your goals to keep going."

First: Who Is the Audience?

Who is the primary audience for this message?

Step 1: Choice

What rhetorical choice does the message make?

Step 2: Audience

Why might that reframing matter specifically to students who are hesitant to seek help?

Step 3: Human Response

What is the writer banking on about people?

Step 4: Effect

How could this human response help the tutoring center accomplish its purpose?

Pathos Can Work Through Self-Perception

Choice → Audience → Human Response → Effect

The message reframes help-seeking as persistence rather than failure → the audience includes students who may hesitate because asking for help can feel like admitting weakness → people often want to maintain a positive view of themselves and may become more open to an action when it fits that self-image → students may therefore become more willing to seek tutoring.

Pathos does not have to mean fear, sadness, or a dramatic story. Writers can appeal to emotions such as pride, reassurance, hope, belonging, frustration, confidence, or concern. The analytical question remains the same: why might this emotional response matter to this audience?

Scenario 4: Appeals Get Messy

One Message, Several Things Happening

A college is promoting its campus food pantry to students. The message says: "Last semester, the pantry provided over 2,000 meals to students. It's staffed by trained student services employees, and all visits are confidential. College is demanding enough without worrying about where your next meal is coming from."

First: Who Is the Audience?

Who is the primary audience for this message?

Step 1: Choice

This time, several rhetorical choices are working together. Which answer best describes what the writer is doing across the whole message?

Step 2: Audience

Why might this combination of information be useful for this particular audience?

Step 3: Human Response

What is the writer banking on about people when these choices are combined?

Step 4: Effect

How could these responses help the college accomplish its purpose?

Real Messages Rarely Stay in Their Boxes

The number of meals can function as logos because it provides concrete evidence that the pantry is actively used. It may also strengthen ethos by making the program appear established.

The trained staff and confidentiality information can strengthen ethos by addressing trust and professionalism, but they also provide practical information that can support logical decision-making.

The final sentence can work through pathos because it acknowledges the stress and burden of worrying about food while managing college.

Those categories help us notice rhetorical choices, but the more important analytical work is explaining how those choices interact with this audience and why they may help accomplish the writer's purpose.

Before Scenario 5

A Very Important Reality Check

So far, this activity has given you preset answers. That was intentional. You needed a way to practice the reasoning process and receive immediate feedback.

Real rhetorical analysis is messier than this activity.

Ethos, logos, and pathos are not three sealed containers. They overlap. A statistic may provide logical evidence while also making a writer seem knowledgeable. Shared experience may build credibility while also creating an emotional connection. A writer may make one choice that affects an audience in several ways at once.

That means your job in a rhetorical analysis is not to guess the one label your instructor has secretly decided is correct.

Your job is to make a reasonable analytical claim and support it. If you can explain what the writer did, why that choice matters for this audience, what the writer may be banking on about human response, and how that could help accomplish the purpose, you are doing rhetorical analysis.

Don't just label it. Defend your reading of it.
Scenario 5: You're the Rhetorical Analyst

Training Wheels Off

A community college is encouraging students to meet with an academic advisor before registering for next semester. The message says: "You have already put time, energy, and money into your education. Before you choose next semester's classes, take twenty minutes to meet with an advisor. Advisors work with degree requirements every day and can help you make sure the classes you choose actually move you toward your goal."

First: Who Is the Audience?

Who is the primary audience for this message?

Your Turn

There is no single rhetorical appeal you are required to choose here. Read the message again and choose one rhetorical choice that you think is worth analyzing.

There Isn't an Answer Key This Time

You might have focused on the reminder that students have already invested time, energy, and money. You might have focused on the advisor's daily experience with degree requirements. You might have focused on the relatively small request of twenty minutes, or on the language about moving toward a student's goal.

Any of those choices could lead to a defensible rhetorical analysis. They may even overlap across ethos, logos, and pathos.

The question is no longer "Did I guess the right appeal?"

The question is "Can I explain my reasoning?"

Before moving on, look back at what you wrote. Your analysis should create a complete chain:

Choice → Audience → Human Response → Effect
