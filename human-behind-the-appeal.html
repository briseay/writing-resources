The Human Behind the Appeal

Rhetorical analysis is not just about finding ethos, logos, and pathos. It is about understanding why a rhetorical choice might work on an actual human being.

Scenario 1 of 5

Before You Begin

You already know how to identify ethos, logos, and pathos. In this activity, we are going one step further.

When writers make rhetorical choices, they are making assumptions about the people they hope to persuade. Your job as a rhetorical analyst is to uncover those assumptions.

Choice → Audience → Human Response → Effect

Another way to think about it is to ask: What is the writer banking on about people?

Each scenario will begin by asking you to identify the audience. Keep that audience in mind as you work. A rhetorical choice only makes sense when we consider who is receiving it.

Scenario 1: Training Wheels

Relevant Expertise

A pediatrician is speaking to a group of parents about childhood nutrition. Early in the presentation, she explains that she has practiced pediatric medicine for 18 years.

First: Who Is the Audience?

Who is the primary audience in this scenario?

Step 1: Choice

What rhetorical choice did the speaker make?

Step 2: Audience

Why is this particular information relevant to this particular audience?

Step 3: Human Response

What is the speaker banking on about human behavior?

Step 4: Effect

How could this human response help the speaker accomplish her purpose?

You Just Moved from Identification to Analysis

It would have been easy to stop with: "The speaker mentions her medical experience, so this is ethos."

That is identification. It is not yet rhetorical analysis.

Choice → Audience → Human Response → Effect

The speaker establishes her medical experience → she is speaking to parents seeking information about their children's health → people often give greater weight to relevant expertise when dealing with specialized information → the parents may therefore give her recommendations more serious consideration.

That is the move we are practicing.

Scenario 2: Shared Experience

Connection as Credibility

A college graduate is speaking to a group of first-generation college students about adjusting to college. Early in the talk, the speaker says, "When I started college, no one in my family had been through this before. During my first semester, I felt completely lost."

First: Who Is the Audience?

Who is the primary audience in this scenario?

Step 1: Choice

What rhetorical choice did the speaker make?

Step 2: Audience

Why might sharing this experience matter to this audience?

Step 3: Human Response

What is the speaker banking on about human behavior?

Step 4: Effect

How could this response help the speaker accomplish the purpose of helping students adjust to college?

Ethos Does Not Require a Résumé

Choice → Audience → Human Response → Effect

The speaker shares an experience of entering college without family experience to rely on → the audience consists of first-generation college students → people may be more receptive to someone who understands an experience they have faced → the students may therefore be more willing to consider the speaker's advice.

Notice what happened to ethos here.

The speaker never gave us a job title, years of professional experience, or a formal credential. Credibility can also develop through connection. For this audience, shared experience may help establish the speaker as someone worth listening to.

Scenario 3: Reframing an Emotion

Pathos Is More Than Making Someone Sad

A college tutoring center creates the following message for students: "Asking for help doesn't mean you're failing. It means you care enough about your goals to keep going."

First: Who Is the Audience?

Based on the message itself, who seems to be the primary audience?

Step 1: Choice

What does the message do with the idea of asking for help?

Step 2: Audience

Why might this way of describing help matter to students who are struggling?

Step 3: Human Response

What is the writer banking on about people?

Step 4: Effect

How could this response help the tutoring center accomplish its purpose?

Pathos Can Work Through Self-Perception

Choice → Audience → Human Response → Effect

The message connects asking for help with persistence rather than failure → the audience is students struggling academically → people may resist actions that seem to threaten how they see themselves → changing what help-seeking represents may make students more willing to seek tutoring.

Pathos does not have to mean fear, sadness, or a dramatic story. Writers can appeal to pride, reassurance, hope, belonging, confidence, concern, and other emotions connected to how people understand themselves and their situations.

The analytical question remains: why might this emotional response matter to this audience?

Scenario 4: Appeals Get Messy

One Message, Several Things Happening

A college is promoting its campus food pantry to students. The message says: "Last semester, the pantry provided over 2,000 meals to students. It's staffed by trained student services employees, and all visits are confidential. College is demanding enough without worrying about where your next meal is coming from."

First: Who Is the Audience?

Which group is the message most directly trying to reach?

Step 1: Choice

Which description best captures the rhetorical work happening across the message?

Step 2: Audience

Why might the writer combine these different kinds of information for this audience?

Step 3: Human Response

Looking at the message as a whole, what is the writer banking on about people?

Step 4: Effect

Which explanation most fully connects those choices to the college's purpose?

Did Some of Those Wrong Answers Sound Right?

Good. They were supposed to.

Several of the answers you just saw described something the message really does. The difference was that some answers explained one part of the message while another accounted for more of the rhetorical situation.

The number of meals can function as logos because it provides concrete evidence that the pantry is actively used. It might also contribute to ethos by making the program seem established.

The trained staff and confidentiality information can strengthen ethos by addressing trust and professionalism. At the same time, those details provide practical information a student could use when deciding whether to visit.

The final sentence can work through pathos because it acknowledges the burden that food concerns can add to an already demanding situation.

The categories still help us notice rhetorical choices. But now the more important question is: Which interpretation can you support with the rhetorical situation?

Before Scenario 5

A Very Important Reality Check

So far, this activity has given you preset answers. That was intentional. You needed a way to practice the reasoning process and receive immediate feedback.

Real rhetorical analysis is messier than this activity.

Ethos, logos, and pathos are not three sealed containers. They overlap. A statistic may provide logical evidence while also making a writer seem knowledgeable. Shared experience may build credibility while also creating an emotional connection. A single rhetorical choice may affect an audience in several ways at once.

You probably noticed this in Scenario 4. Some of the answers marked incorrect were not ridiculous or completely false. They captured part of what the message was doing. You had to decide which explanation best accounted for the question being asked and the larger rhetorical situation.

That means your job in a rhetorical analysis is not to guess the one label your instructor has secretly decided is correct.

Your job is to make a reasonable analytical claim and support it. If you can explain what the writer did, why that choice matters for this audience, what the writer may be banking on about human response, and how that could help accomplish the purpose, you are doing rhetorical analysis.

Don't just label it. Defend your reading of it.
Scenario 5: You're the Rhetorical Analyst

Training Wheels Off

A community college is encouraging students to meet with an academic advisor before registering for next semester. The message says: "You have already put time, energy, and money into your education. Before you choose next semester's classes, take twenty minutes to meet with an advisor. Advisors work with degree requirements every day and can help you make sure the classes you choose actually move you toward your goal."

First: Who Is the Audience?

Which group of students is the message most directly trying to reach?

Your Turn

There is no single rhetorical appeal you are required to choose here. Read the message again and choose one rhetorical choice that you think is worth analyzing.

You can use ethos, logos, or pathos to help you notice a choice, but your explanation needs to go further than naming the appeal.

There Isn't an Answer Key This Time

You might have focused on the reminder that students have already invested time, energy, and money. You might have focused on the advisor's experience with degree requirements. You might have focused on the relatively small request of twenty minutes, or on the language about moving toward a student's goal.

Any of those choices could lead to a defensible rhetorical analysis. They may even overlap across ethos, logos, and pathos.

The question is no longer "Did I guess the right appeal?"

The question is "Can I explain my reasoning?"

Look back at what you wrote. Your analysis should create a complete chain:
