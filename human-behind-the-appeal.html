The Human Behind the Appeal

Rhetorical analysis is not just about finding ethos, logos, and pathos. It is about understanding why a rhetorical choice might work on an actual human being.

Scenario 1 of 5

Before You Begin

You already know how to identify ethos, logos, and pathos. In this activity, we are going one step further.

When writers make rhetorical choices, they are making assumptions about the people they hope to persuade. Your job as a rhetorical analyst is to uncover those assumptions.

Choice → Audience → Human Response → Effect

Another way to think about it is to ask: What is the writer banking on about people?

Scenario 1: Training Wheels

Relevant Expertise

A pediatrician is speaking to a group of parents about childhood nutrition. Early in the presentation, she explains that she has practiced pediatric medicine for 18 years.

First: Who Is the Audience?

Before analyzing any rhetorical choice, make sure you know who the speaker is trying to reach. Who is the primary audience in this scenario?

Step 1: Choice

What rhetorical choice did the speaker make?

Step 2: Audience

Why is this particular information relevant to this particular audience?

Step 3: Human Response

What is the speaker banking on about human behavior?

Step 4: Effect

How could this human response help the speaker accomplish her purpose?

You Just Moved from Identification to Analysis

At the beginning, it would have been easy to stop here: "The speaker mentions her medical experience, so this is ethos."

That is identification. It is not yet rhetorical analysis.

Choice → Audience → Human Response → Effect

The speaker establishes her medical experience → she is speaking to parents seeking information about their children's health → people often give greater trust to someone with relevant expertise when dealing with specialized information → the parents may therefore be more willing to trust and seriously consider her recommendations.

That is the move we are practicing.
