MLA Style

MLA style provides a consistent system for formatting academic papers, crediting source material in the body of an essay, and identifying sources on a Works Cited page.

Setting Up the Document

Formatting Your Paper

MLA formatting creates a consistent, readable document. Unless your assignment instructions say otherwise, use the following basic requirements.

Font and Spacing

Use 12-point Times New Roman font and double-space the entire document.

Margins

Use one-inch margins on all sides of the document.

Paragraphs

Indent the first line of each paragraph. Do not add extra spaces before or after paragraphs.

Page Numbers

Include your last name and the page number in the upper-right corner.

Paper Information

Include your name, professor's name, course title, and assignment due date at the beginning of the paper.

Title

Center the paper title. Do not automatically bold, underline, or enlarge it.

Writing Process Reminder

Editing and revising are not the same task.

Editing addresses local, mechanical concerns such as typos, word choice, commas, semicolons, run-ons, and fragments.

Revising addresses the content of the paper, including clarity, concision, evidence, logic, and organization.

Quick Tip

Formatting should be completed before submission, but formatting alone cannot repair an underdeveloped essay. Revise the content first, then complete the final editing and MLA check.

Crediting Sources in the Essay

In-Text Citations

When you use a source's ideas, summaries, paraphrases, or direct quotations, MLA format requires you to credit the source in the body of the paper.

The information in the in-text citation should match the first element of the source's Works Cited entry. This is usually the author's last name.

Why This Matters

In-text citations show readers where source-based information begins and help them locate the complete publication information on the Works Cited page.

One Author

(Author's Last Name page number)

Many students struggle to balance full-time jobs with college coursework (Lopez 14).

Lopez argues that “students need stronger financial support” (14).

Two Authors

(First Author and Second Author page number)

Studies show a link between screen time and anxiety (Jenkins and Moore 245).

Jenkins and Moore found that “digital overuse correlates with decreased well-being” (245).

Three or More Authors

(First Author et al. page number)

Collaborative learning increases motivation in online classes (Nguyen et al. 78).

Nguyen et al. found that “peer feedback strengthens confidence” (78).

No Page Number

(Author's Last Name)

When an online source does not provide page numbers, use the author's last name only.

When paragraph or section numbers are provided, those may be used, such as (par. 4).

Quick Reminders

  • Do not place a comma between the author's name and page number.
  • Do not use p. or pg. before the page number.
  • If the author's name appears in your sentence, do not repeat it in parentheses.
  • Do not include the author's first name in the parenthetical citation.
  • Place the citation before the sentence period.
  • Every in-text citation must match a Works Cited entry.
Compare the Two Citation Patterns

Author Named in the Sentence

According to Brown, “Native American women who live on reservations experience sexual assault at a higher rate than women who do not live on reservations” (93).

Because Brown is named in the sentence, only the page number appears in parentheses.

Author Not Named in the Sentence

Due to federal and state regulations, “Native American women who live on reservations experience sexual assault at a higher rate than women who do not live on reservations” (Brown 93).

Because Brown is not named in the sentence, both the author's last name and page number appear in parentheses.

Integrating Source Evidence

Using Quotes Effectively

Quoting brings another writer's exact words into your paper. The quote should support your own idea, not take over the paragraph.

When to Use a Quote

  • The wording is powerful or memorable.
  • The author expresses the idea especially effectively.
  • You want to add authority and evidence to support your point.

Avoid Drop Quotes

A quotation should never appear by itself as a complete sentence in your paragraph. Connect it grammatically to your own writing by tagging or blending it.

Drop Quote

“Native American women who live on reservations experience sexual assault at a higher rate than women who do not live on reservations” (Brown 93).

Integrated Quote

According to Brown, “Native American women who live on reservations experience sexual assault at a higher rate than women who do not live on reservations” (93).

Two Ways to Set Up a Quote

Tagging

Introduce the author before the quotation.

According to Brown, “Native American women who live on reservations experience sexual assault at a higher rate than women who do not live on reservations” (93).

Blending

Introduce the information first and blend the quotation into your own sentence.

Due to federal and state regulations, “Native American women who live on reservations experience sexual assault at a higher rate than women who do not live on reservations” (Brown 93).

Grammar Check

Your sentence should remain grammatically correct when the quotation is inserted. A partial quotation must fit smoothly into the grammar of your sentence.

Using Ellipses and Brackets

Ellipsis

Use an ellipsis to show that words have been removed from the middle of a quotation.

Green claims that “bombs in Central Europe were completely unreal to us…because our place was too fair for us to accept something like that” (23).

Brackets

Use brackets to make a small grammatical change, such as adjusting a pronoun or verb tense. Do not change the original meaning.

Green claims that “bombs in Central Europe were completely unreal to [them]…because [their] place was too fair for [them] to accept something like that” (23).

The Double Down Rule

Think of a quote like a guest at your table. You have to respond to it, not just sit in silence.

If your quotation is one sentence long, write at least two sentences explaining, analyzing, or connecting it to your argument.

Your voice should always be louder than the source's voice. The quote provides evidence and credibility, but your explanation shows that you understand the topic.

Why This Matters

Quotations should support your ideas rather than replace them. A paragraph filled with quotations may sound like a collection of other people's ideas instead of your own argument.

Documenting Sources

Building a Works Cited Page

The Works Cited page lists every source used in the essay. It gives readers the publication information needed to locate each source and increases the writer's credibility.

If a source is not cited in the essay, do not include it on the Works Cited page. Every in-text citation should match an entry on this page.

Credibility Matters

A complete and accurate Works Cited page strengthens your credibility. An incomplete or error-filled Works Cited page can damage it.

Core Elements of an MLA Entry

MLA entries generally follow this order. Include the elements that are available for your source.

  1. Author.
  2. “Title of Source.” This may be an article, essay, web page, poem, short story, or another shorter work.
  3. Title of Container, such as the book, journal, magazine, database, or website containing the source.
  4. Other Contributors, such as editors or translators, when applicable.
  5. Version, such as an edition or version number.
  6. Number, such as a volume or issue number.
  7. Publisher, when applicable.
  8. Publication Date,
  9. Location. This may include a page range, DOI, or URL.

Online Sources

Include a DOI for a source found online when one is available. If no DOI is available, include the URL.

Journal Article Pattern

Last Name, First Name. “Title of the Article in Title Case.” Title of the Journal, vol. #, no. #, year, pp. range, DOI or URL.

Journal Article Example

Davis, Deena S. “Advance Directives and Alzheimer's Disease.” Journal of Law, Medicine, & Ethics, vol. 46, no. 3, 2018, pp. 744-748, https://doi.org/10.1177/1073110518804235.

Breaking Down the Entry

Author Davis, Deena S.
Article Title “Advance Directives and Alzheimer's Disease.”
Journal Title Journal of Law, Medicine, & Ethics,
Publication Information vol. 46, no. 3, 2018, pp. 744-748,
DOI https://doi.org/10.1177/1073110518804235.

Formatting Authors' Names

Situation Rule Example
One Author Write the last name first, followed by the first name. Smith, John.
Two Authors Reverse only the first author's name and connect the names with “and.” Smith, John, and Mary Johnson.
Three or More Authors Write the first author's name followed by et al. Smith, John, et al.
Middle Names Spell out the first and last names. Use initials for middle names when provided. Smith, John R.

Titles and Punctuation

  • Put quotation marks around shorter works, such as articles, essays, poems, web pages, and short stories.
  • Italicize longer works and containers, such as books, journals, magazines, databases, and websites.
  • Capitalize the major words in titles.

Example

“Education and Inequality.” Journal of Social Research,

Formatting the Page

  • Begin the Works Cited page on a new page.
  • Center the heading Works Cited.
  • List entries alphabetically by the first author's last name.
  • Do not rearrange the authors within a source.
  • Double-space the entire page.
  • Do not add extra spacing between entries.
  • Use a half-inch hanging indent for every line after the first line of each entry.

Hanging Indent

The first line of a Works Cited entry begins at the left margin. Every additional line in that same entry is indented one-half inch.

Troubleshooting

Common MLA Mistakes

Using p. or pg.

MLA in-text citations use the page number without p. or pg.

Putting the Citation After the Period

The citation normally appears before the sentence period.

Repeating the Author

When the author's name is already in the sentence, include only the page number in parentheses.

Using the Author's First Name

Parenthetical citations use the author's last name, not the first name.

Dropping a Quote

Blend or tag the quotation so it becomes part of your own grammatically complete sentence.

Skipping the Explanation

Use the Double Down Rule to explain how the quotation supports your claim.

Mismatched Citations

The in-text citation must match the first element of the Works Cited entry.

Including Unused Sources

Only list sources that are actually cited in the essay.

Extra Paragraph Spacing

Double-space the paper, but do not add extra blank space between paragraphs or Works Cited entries.

Missing the Hanging Indent

Indent all lines after the first line of each Works Cited entry.

Before You Submit

Final MLA Checklist

Use this checklist after revising and editing your paper.

Paper Formatting

  • My paper uses 12-point Times New Roman font.
  • My entire paper is double-spaced.
  • My paper has one-inch margins.
  • I removed extra spacing between paragraphs.
  • Each paragraph begins with an indentation.
  • My last name and page number appear in the upper-right corner.
  • My name, professor, course, and due date appear at the beginning.
  • My title is centered.

In-Text Citations

  • I cited all summaries, paraphrases, and direct quotations.
  • My citations use the author's last name and page number when available.
  • I did not use a comma between the name and page number.
  • I did not use p. or pg. before page numbers.
  • My citations appear before the sentence period.
  • I did not repeat the author's name in parentheses when it already appears in the sentence.

Quotation Integration

  • I did not use any drop quotes.
  • Each quotation fits grammatically into my sentence.
  • I used brackets or ellipses only when needed and did not change the source's meaning.
  • I explained how each quotation supports my own idea.
  • My own analysis is more prominent than the source quotations.

Works Cited

  • My Works Cited page begins on a new page.
  • The heading Works Cited is centered.
  • My entries are in alphabetical order.
  • The page is double-spaced.
  • I did not add extra spaces between entries.
  • Each entry uses a hanging indent.
  • Short works use quotation marks.
  • Containers and longer works use italics.
  • I included a DOI or URL for online sources.
  • Every Works Cited entry matches an in-text citation in my essay.

Final Reminder

Always follow the directions for your current assignment. A course prompt or instructor may include requirements beyond these general MLA guidelines.
